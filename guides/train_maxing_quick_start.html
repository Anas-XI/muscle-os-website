Train Maxing is the practice of systematically organising your training around split selection, volume management, and progressive overload to produce consistent, measurable results. This guide distils the essential principles into five actionable steps so you can start training with purpose today. This and related topics are covered in depth in the Training & Programming Book.
Step 1

Pick Your Split

Your training split determines how you organise your training days. Choose based on how many days you can train and what you want to achieve.

Available DaysRecommended SplitBest For
2 daysFull BodyTime-constrained, novice lifters
3 daysFull Body or Push/Pull/LegsGeneral fitness, moderate availability
4 daysUpper / LowerBest balance of frequency and recovery
5 daysPush / Pull / Legs or Upper/Lower+PPL hybridHigh commitment, advanced lifters
6 daysPush / Pull / Legs or Bro SplitMaximum volume, bodybuilding focus
Coach says: More days is not always better. Four focused sessions beat six rushed ones. Pick the split that fits your schedule, not the one that sounds most impressive.
Step 2

Set Your Training Frequency

Frequency = how many times per week each muscle group gets trained. This determines recovery and growth potential.

Split TypeFrequency per Muscle Group
Full Body (2-3 days)2-3x per week
Upper / Lower (4 days)2x per week
Push / Pull / Legs (5-6 days)2x per week (some groups 1.5x)
Bro Split (6 days)1x per week (high volume each session)

Key rule: Higher frequency (2-3x/week) with moderate volume per session is superior for hypertrophy compared to lower frequency (1x/week) with high volume per session. The MPS window lasts 24-48 hours — more frequent stimulation keeps it elevated.

Coach says: Train each muscle group at least 2x per week if possible. Unless you're advanced, 1x/week frequency leaves gains on the table.
Step 3

Step 4

Track Progressive Overload

Every session should be slightly harder than last time. If you're not progressing, you're not growing.

The Double Progression Method

  1. Pick a rep range (e.g., 8-12 reps for hypertrophy).
  2. Stay at the same weight until you can hit the top of the range (12 reps) with good form on your first set.
  3. Increase the weight by the smallest increment available (2.5-5 kg).
  4. Repeat — you'll likely drop back to 8 reps. Build back up to 12.

What to Track

Track ThisWhyHow Often
Weight on the barPrimary overload variableEvery session
Reps achievedTells you when to increase weightEvery set
Sets completedVolume trackingEvery session
RPE of each setEnsures you're in the right effort zoneEvery set
Coach says: Never increase weight at the expense of form. If you can't control the rep, the weight is too heavy. Progress = quality reps over time.
Step 5

Know When to Deload

Deloading is planned recovery. It prevents burnout and keeps you progressing long-term.

When to Deload

  • Every 4-8 weeks as a proactive maintenance week.
  • When performance stalls for 2+ weeks despite good adherence.
  • When sleep quality drops, motivation disappears, or joints hurt.

How to Deload

MethodSetsIntensityDuration
Volume deload40-60% of normalSame or slightly lower5-7 days
Intensity deloadSame as normal50-70% of normal5-7 days
Full restZeroZero3-5 days
Active recoveryLight movement onlyLight cardio, mobility5-7 days
Coach says: A deload is not a vacation — it's maintenance. You are not losing gains. You are shedding accumulated fatigue so you can smash your next block. Use the Deload Decision Tree to decide when.

Common Mistakes

Avoid these. They will cost you weeks of progress.

  • Adding too much volume too fast — your MRV is lower than you think. Fatigue accumulates silently for 3-4 weeks before you crash.
  • Never training close to failure — if every set has 4+ reps in reserve, you're doing glorified cardio. Intensity drives adaptation.
  • Changing splits every 4 weeks — pick a split and run it for 8-16 weeks. Consistency of programming is how you measure progress.
  • Ignoring the deload — "I don't need a deload, I'll just push through" is exactly what people say before getting injured or overtrained.
  • Not tracking your lifts — if you don't know what you did last session, you're guessing. Guessing is not programming. Use a log, an app, or a notebook.
